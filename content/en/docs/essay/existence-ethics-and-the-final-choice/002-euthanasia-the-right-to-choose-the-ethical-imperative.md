# Euthanasia. The Right to Choose. The Ethical Imperative | Existence Ethics And The Final Choice

![_Euthanasia. The Right to Choose. The Ethical Imperative | Existence Ethics And The Final Choice. AP | Pivtorak.Studio. 09.11.2025_ © Anna Pivtorak (Kostyuk)](/images/essay-existence-ethics-and-the-final-choice-002-euthanasia-the-right-to-choose-the-ethical-imperative.webp)

The subject of euthanasia has always evoked mixed feelings, ranging from anxiety to relief. My first associations are connected with human dignity, the limits of suffering, and the right of individuals to participate in determining the course of their own lives. In a world where medicine can technologically postpone death, the question of ending suffering acquires a new and profound ethical dimension.

My personal view is that euthanasia, under clearly defined legal and medical conditions, can be a necessary means of ending unbearable suffering for a person who consciously and voluntarily expresses such a wish. The question becomes particularly complex when the prognosis is hopeless and the possibilities of recovery and relief have been exhausted. A number of countries have established legal frameworks for this practice. At the same time, refusing futile treatment and withdrawing artificial life support are distinct medical practices and should not automatically be classified as euthanasia. This is why clear legal distinctions are essential, particularly for protecting patients' rights and providing legal certainty for medical professionals.

A deeper understanding of this subject requires distinguishing between different medical and ethical practices. Refusing treatment that no longer offers a reasonable prospect of benefit, or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment under appropriate circumstances, does not constitute euthanasia. These may be justified decisions when recovery is no longer possible and further interventions merely prolong the dying process. Palliative care, in turn, should provide the greatest possible relief from physical and psychological suffering. Quality of life, dignity, and the patient's wishes must be integral to medical decisions rather than subordinated to an unconditional imperative to prolong biological existence at any cost. When a person is no longer capable of expressing their wishes, their previously stated preferences, lawful representation arrangements, and best interests must be taken into account.

The question becomes considerably more complex when voluntary euthanasia is considered in the context of incurable conditions accompanied by prolonged and unbearable suffering, including psychological suffering. Emotional and psychological pain can be profoundly destructive even when it has no visible physical manifestation. This is precisely where particular caution is necessary. A thorough assessment, access to qualified psychiatric and psychological care, sustained support, and genuine opportunities for social integration and rehabilitation are essential. Participation in support and mentoring programmes may open new perspectives, help individuals rediscover meaning, and reveal possibilities for a different future. The ability to help others can sometimes become a new mission, but no one should be required to prove their worth through their usefulness to society.

The cultural and ethical dimensions of this discussion also encompass the concepts of "mercy killing" and "physician-assisted suicide". However, these terms are not interchangeable with euthanasia. Wartime situations are particularly difficult, especially when they involve the question of ending the unbearable suffering of a severely wounded soldier. Compassion, human dignity, military duty, medical ethics, and the obligation to preserve life intersect in such circumstances. They require separate moral and legal consideration.

The right to die, in the context of euthanasia, remains the subject of complex ethical debate. It cannot be considered in isolation from the right to life, access to healthcare, palliative support, and freedom from coercion. Any decision-making process must safeguard voluntariness, informed consent, protection from pressure, and respect for human dignity. Society must create conditions in which people can receive appropriate assistance, understand the available alternatives, and avoid being left alone with their suffering.

From a religious or philosophical perspective, the concept of the "sanctity of life" may represent a fundamental value. Yet it does not always provide an answer to someone experiencing unbearable pain. Self-determination is one of the fundamental human freedoms, although its limits and its relationship with society's responsibilities remain subjects of debate. Society should never leave a person with nothing but desperate attempts to end their suffering alone. It must provide dignified care, access to treatment, and the possibility of making an informed choice without coercion.

At the same time, when someone expresses a wish to die simply because they believe their life's mission has been completed, this deserves particular attention. A sense of completion does not necessarily mean that no future remains. Sometimes it signals profound exhaustion, a loss of direction, or the need to reconsider one's existence. Perhaps, at that moment, what a person needs is not a new responsibility, but support, space to recover, and the opportunity to discover a different perspective. The completion of one mission does not necessarily mean the end of life's journey.

From the perspective of my understanding of the soul's journey, the voluntary choice of the moment of death also carries a metaphysical dimension. Within my worldview, an early departure from earthly life may signify that certain spiritual lessons remain unfinished. I consider it possible that, in such circumstances, the soul may remain in the Subtle World, not inwardly prepared either to return to another incarnation or to merge with the Absolute. From this perspective, unresolved questions may reappear in a subsequent life in a different, perhaps more challenging, form, and the spiritual journey may require the assistance of descendants to achieve union with Eternal Energy. This is my personal metaphysical interpretation, not a universal explanation of human death.

> 🖋️ _"The right to die is not a rejection of life, but a search for dignity where pain overshadows everything else."_

# The Ethics of Suffering and Care | Existence Ethics and The Final Choice

![_The Ethics of Suffering and Care | Existence Ethics and The Final Choice. AP | Pivtorak.Studio. 17.11.2025_ © Anna Pivtorak (Kostyuk)](/images/essay-existence-ethics-and-the-final-choice-003-the-ethics-of-suffering-and-care.webp)

The ethics of suffering is one of those subjects on which social judgments often sound particularly cruel and superficial. My first associations are not with morality, but with hypocrisy: people allow themselves to condemn medical professionals who save lives and relieve pain, yet often avoid even witnessing the genuine suffering of their own loved ones. **Who, exactly, are we entitled to judge?**

My personal experience changed me forever. When my mother's cancer returned, her request was singular and unequivocal: she did not want to be hospitalized. She wanted no unfamiliar walls — only her own home, her own dignity, and silence. Calling the palliative care service became a critically important step. These professionals helped me recognize something I had initially been unable to see for myself: **silent suffering**, concealed in laboured breathing. After that, I learned to administer injections, kept a record of every procedure, coordinated my actions with the doctor, and stayed by her side until the very last second. It was my moral duty, both as a daughter and as a human being.

At a deeper level, palliative care is one of the central elements in the discussion of the right to die. It does more than relieve pain: it helps patients and their families understand the course of an illness, its prognosis, and the realistic options for treatment. It creates space for decisions that may seem frightening but above all require humanity and responsibility. At the same time, palliative care, the withdrawal or refusal of life-sustaining treatment, and euthanasia must be distinguished from one another. They are different practices, although all raise the difficult question of the limits of medical intervention and a person's right to dignity at the end of life. If society recognizes the possibility of voluntary medically assisted ending of life, it must establish a clear protocol that protects both patients and medical professionals: ensuring informed and voluntary consent, freedom from coercion, an appropriate assessment of the person's condition, and independent oversight.

Society must also acknowledge the reality of **limited medical resources**. Their allocation should be based on medical indications, urgency, and the expected benefit of treatment. Helping those who can be saved and caring for those who can no longer be cured should not be treated as opposing priorities. Palliative care is no less important a component of the healthcare system. Society's responsibility is not only to save lives, but also to ensure that no one is left alone with their suffering.

In the cultural and social context, it is important to distinguish physical suffering from suffering associated with severe mental disorders. Psychological pain can intensify through loneliness, isolation, and the absence of sustained human support — the presence of someone who can listen, share experience, and serve as a mentor. Antidepressants may be an essential part of treatment, but they do not cure loneliness or replace human relationships. This is why people experiencing emotional suffering also need **a dedicated mentoring system** — one that is sustained, accessible, and built on trust. It should complement professional psychological and psychiatric care and help people find their way back to a fulfilling life.

For me, the ethics of suffering is not about death. It is about whether we are capable of being there. Whether we are capable of facing the truth. Whether we understand that **a person's dignity is measured not only by how we value their life, but also by how we remain beside them until the very end**.

> 🖋️ *"Dignified care is not only about medicine. It is about the ability to be there for someone."*

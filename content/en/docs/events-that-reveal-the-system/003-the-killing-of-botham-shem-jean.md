# The Killing of Botham Shem Jean | Events That Reveal the System

![_The Killing of Botham Shem Jean | Events That Reveal the System. AP | Pivtorak.Studio. 27.11.2026_ © Anna Pivtorak (Kostyuk)](/images/events-that-reveal-the-system-003-the-killing-of-botham-shem-jean.webp)

**Dallas, Texas · September 6, 2018**

_At the Threshold of Someone Else’s Home_

**One tragedy — several levels of responsibility**

On the evening of September 6, 2018, in Dallas, Texas, police officer Amber Renee Guyger entered Botham Shem Jean’s apartment and shot him.

She claimed that she had confused the apartments and believed she had returned to her own home. Botham Jean was at home, relaxing, watching television and eating ice cream.

In October 2019, a jury found Guyger guilty of murder. She was sentenced to ten years in prison.

This story raises many questions. How could a police officer make such a serious mistake? Why did she use her weapon without first confirming that she was in her own apartment? Could professional training have helped her avoid this mistake? Were there conditions in the building that contributed to the confusion? Did the people and organizations responsible for safety fulfil their obligations?

We will not look for excuses in the circumstances. But neither will we limit ourselves to the court’s verdict.

Our aim is to examine the event on three levels:

- the personal responsibility of the person who fired the shots;  
- the responsibility of the police organization;  
- the responsibility of the system responsible for the safety of the residential building.  

We are examining two systems not to shift the blame for the killing onto them, but to understand which failures could have been prevented.

## Section I. Individual Responsibility

### A decision made by one person

Amber Renee Guyger was a police officer. She had professional training, wore a uniform, carried a service weapon and had powers that an ordinary citizen does not possess.

But that evening, she was not on an official assignment. She was returning home.

She confused the floors and the apartments. Importantly, her electronic key did not confirm that she had access to the apartment. A subsequent examination established that her key fob did not unlock Jean’s apartment door.

This was a circumstance that should have prompted her to stop and check.

However, Guyger did not stop to establish whether she was actually in her own apartment. She saw a person in a dark room, drew her weapon and opened fire.

The television was on in the apartment. Botham Jean was at home, wearing ordinary casual clothes. The things around him were consistent with an ordinary evening: a sofa, a table, a television, food and ice cream.

None of these circumstances gave her the right to shoot a person.

We cannot know exactly what was going through Guyger’s mind at that moment. Her psychological state cannot be established solely from her appearance, facial expression or assumptions about her private life.

But we can examine her specific actions and the sequence of decisions she made.

Why did she not check the apartment? Why did she not use the lighting to assess the situation more clearly? Why did she not stop after her key failed to work? Why did she use lethal force without establishing that a real threat existed?

These questions concern her personal decisions.

**The circumstances surrounding the event did not make the decision on her behalf.**

### Responsibility must not dissolve into circumstances

A long working shift, professional stress, organizational culture, access to a weapon and other circumstances may all be subjects of investigation.

However, none of them automatically explains or justifies the shooting.

Even if the employer had organized working hours poorly, even if professional training had shortcomings, even if the building had defective doors, none of this removes a person's responsibility for their actions.

The jury found Guyger guilty of murder. This is an established judicial fact and the starting point of our analysis.

We are not conducting a second trial. We are examining what a verdict alone cannot repair.

## Section II. Systemic Responsibility

A systemic failure is not necessarily one major malfunction. Sometimes it consists of many smaller shortcomings that exist side by side, remain insufficiently addressed and can create dangerous conditions.

In this case, we see two systems with different responsibilities.

### System No. 1. The Police Organization

A police organization is more than a group of people in uniform. It is an employer that recruits personnel, trains them, grants them authority, issues weapons, determines work schedules, oversees professional conduct and is responsible for organizing police service.

Training should not end when an officer graduates from the academy.

A person may acquire the necessary knowledge and skills at the beginning of their career, but this does not mean they will maintain an appropriate level of professional readiness over the following years.

Regular training, practical exercises, analysis of difficult situations, skills assessments and evaluation of decision-making are necessary.

It is particularly important to train police officers not only to act, but also to recognize when to pause, verify information, question their first impressions and avoid unjustified use of force.

#### Professional confidence and the sense of being infallible

Police officers have powers that ordinary citizens do not possess. The uniform, service weapon, training and authority to require compliance with lawful orders place them in a position of particular responsibility.

But professional confidence must not turn into a personal sense of infallibility.

Police officers must be able to question their own assumptions. They need to understand that they can make mistakes, misjudge a situation or lack sufficient information.

That is why training should include the analysis of real cases, video footage, difficult situations and alternative decisions.

It is important to examine not only mistakes, but also correct actions that helped prevent the use of force.

#### Working hours and professional readiness

On the day of the tragedy, Guyger had worked approximately 13 hours and 46 minutes.

This fact does not prove that fatigue caused the shooting. But it raises an important question: how does an organization account for an employee’s condition after a prolonged shift, including the transition from professional duties back to private life?

Work schedules, overtime, rest periods between shifts and employees’ overall condition should all be part of the safety system.

An employer should not assess only the number of hours worked. It should also consider how working conditions affect a person’s everyday life and ability to recover after work that involves responsibility for other people's lives.

#### Physical and psychological preparation

Police training cannot be limited to knowledge of the law and the ability to use a weapon.

It requires continuous work on physical fitness, psychological resilience, self-control and the ability to act under pressure.

We propose treating regular physical activity, practical firearms training, scenario-based exercises and psychological support as components of professional readiness.

It is also important to study why people choose a career in policing, what motivates them to remain in the profession, and what causes dissatisfaction, fatigue and professional burnout.

These studies should not be one-off exercises. A person's professional development continues throughout their career.

#### Relationships between colleagues

Personal relationships between employees can affect team dynamics, professional decisions and independence when carrying out official duties.

In the Guyger case, her personal relationship with a colleague, Martin Rivera, who was married, also came to light.

We are not claiming that this relationship caused the tragedy. But it provides grounds for asking how personal relationships within the police service should be managed.

An organization should have clear rules for situations in which personal connections may affect professional activities, lines of authority, the allocation of duties or the objectivity of decisions.

#### Weapons and the end of the professional role

A separate issue is the carrying of a service weapon after the end of a working shift.

A police firearm is a tool for carrying out professional duties. It should not automatically accompany a person into every area of private life.

We propose considering a clear separation between professional and private roles:

- after completing a shift, an employee changes out of their service uniform;  
- the service weapon is stored in a properly equipped secure safe at the workplace;  
- carrying a service weapon outside working hours is permitted only in specifically defined work-related circumstances;  
- the rules governing such exceptions must be clear and subject to oversight.  

This does not mean that a police officer stops being a responsible citizen after the end of a shift. The point is to ensure that official authority and a service weapon do not become an automatic extension of private life.

#### Independent oversight and professional well-being

A police organization should not only demand discipline; it should also assess how effectively it fulfils its own responsibilities.

Independent assessment, regular analysis of professional risks, access to psychological support after critical incidents and evaluation of training quality are necessary.

Psychological assessment should be a regular part of an officer’s professional life, not a procedure carried out only at the point of recruitment. We propose using the polygraph as an additional assessment tool alongside psychological tests, interviews and other evaluation methods. Its use should be routine and independent of any personal relationship between the employee and the examiner. It is important not to limit the process to an isolated result, but to accumulate and analyse information over time in order to better understand changes in employees’ professional condition, behaviour and motivation.

A police organization should be more than an employer that demands the completion of tasks. It should be a system that continuously works to improve the quality of its own performance.

#### Questions for the police organization

To understand how effectively a police organization fulfils its responsibilities, it is not enough to know that its employees attend training and pass examinations. We need to examine how the organization maintains an individual's professional readiness throughout their career.

We propose asking the following questions.

**Recruitment and professional development**

- What criteria are used to select candidates for police service?  
- How does the organization assess not only physical and psychological readiness, but also a person's ability to think critically, question their own decisions and regulate their behaviour?  
- Does professional training continue after graduation from the academy?  
- How often do police officers undergo refresher training and practical exercises?  
- Do rank-and-file officers have genuine opportunities to pursue further education and professional development?   
- Are these opportunities available not only to managers and employees in office-based departments?

**Use of firearms and professional readiness**

- How often are officers’ safe firearms-handling skills assessed?  
- Does training include scenarios in which the correct decision is not to use a weapon?  
- Are police officers trained to pause, verify the circumstances and avoid acting on the basis of their first impression?  
- Are cases of mistaken use of force and opportunities to prevent them analysed?  
- Is an employee’s ability to maintain self-control in stressful situations assessed?  

**Working hours and workload**

- What limits are in place for the length of shifts and overtime?  
- What minimum rest period must be provided between shifts?  
- Does the organization assess the impact of prolonged working hours on employees’ well-being and everyday lives?  
- Who is responsible for decisions concerning an employee's readiness to continue working after a prolonged shift?  
- Is accumulated workload taken into account, rather than only the duration of the most recent shift?  

**Psychological well-being and organizational culture**

- Does the organization study why people choose policing as a profession and what motivates them to remain in the service?  
- Are regular assessments of job satisfaction, stress and burnout carried out?  
- Is psychological support available after critical incidents?  
- Can employees report problems without fear of punishment or negative consequences for their careers?  
- Does the organization examine its own culture and attitudes towards authority, weapons and the use of force?  

**Personal relationships and professional independence**

- Are there rules governing personal relationships between colleagues?  
- How does the organization prevent conflicts of interest within the same department?  
- Can personal relationships influence the formation of working partnerships, shifts and the allocation of duties?  
- What measures are taken when such relationships create risks for professional activities?  

**Professional and private roles**

- Should service weapons remain at the organization after the end of a shift?   
- Are changing out of uniform and a clear end to the professional role part of the procedure?  
- Under what circumstances may a police officer carry a service weapon outside working hours?   
- Are there clear rules and oversight mechanisms for such exceptions?  
- How does the organization distinguish between an employee’s official authority and private life?  

**Oversight and employer responsibility**

- Who independently assesses the quality of police training and officers’ professional readiness?  
- How often is the effectiveness of internal oversight evaluated?  
- Are recurring mistakes and previous incidents analysed?  
- Does the organization check whether recommendations made after internal investigations have been implemented?  
- What are the consequences when the organization fails to fulfil its own obligations?  
- Is there an independent mechanism for evaluating the performance of the police organization?  

These questions do not mean that all the problems listed necessarily exist in every police organization. They identify what needs to be examined in order to assess the quality of management, training, oversight and employer accountability.

### System No. 2. Residential Infrastructure

The second system in this case is the residential building, its maintenance, management and the safety of its residents.

At first glance, it is an ordinary apartment building. But its condition and the way its space is organized also matter for people's safety.

#### Similar apartments and navigation

Apartments in the same building may have similar layouts, identical doors, similar corridors and matching design features.

This can create a risk of confusion, particularly when floor markings and apartment numbers are not sufficiently visible.

Residential infrastructure should therefore provide clear navigation: residents and visitors should be able to identify easily which floor they are on and which apartment door they are standing in front of.

This does not justify entering someone else's apartment by mistake, but it is one of the factors that should be considered when designing and maintaining buildings.

#### Doors and locks

The investigation into the tragedy established that Jean’s apartment door had technical problems with closing properly.

The case materials also referred to residents’ reports that the door did not always close correctly.

A technical examination identified defects in the door frame and the metal strike plate of the lock. An experiment demonstrated that the door could fail to latch in the closed position.

This is no longer a question of how similar the apartments looked. It is a question of building maintenance and residential safety.

When residents report a problem with a door, building management should do more than simply receive the complaint. It should ensure that the problem is inspected and resolved.

#### Questions for the residential infrastructure system

- When did management first receive reports of the defect?  
- Were repair requests formally registered?  
- Who was responsible for inspecting the door?  
- Were follow-up inspections carried out?  
- Had the defect been repaired before the tragedy?  

The existence of a technical defect does not, by itself, answer all these questions. It is necessary to establish what management knew, when it became aware of the problem and how it responded.

But the very existence of the defect already demonstrates how important proper residential building maintenance is.

#### Residents’ safety is not a secondary service

People should feel safe in their own homes.

Secure doors, functioning locks, corridor lighting, clear navigation and timely repairs are not additional benefits of housing. They are essential components of its proper operation.

If building management receives payment for maintenance, it must fulfil the corresponding obligations.

Residents are expected to pay their bills on time. But management must also respond to faults in a timely manner.

It cannot be right to demand perfect financial discipline from an individual while the organization's own fulfilment of its obligations remains inadequately monitored.

## Conclusion. Responsibility Must Not Disappear at Any Level

The case of Botham Shem Jean presents a tragedy in which we must distinguish between an individual's actions and the responsibilities of organizations.

Amber Renee Guyger was found guilty of murder. Systemic analysis does not change this fact or diminish her personal responsibility.

But a verdict does not repair doors, change police service regulations, improve training or create mechanisms to prevent future tragedies.

That is precisely why we examine two systems.

The police organization must be accountable for the people it recruits, trains, arms and grants authority to.

Residential infrastructure must be accountable for the technical condition of the building, the reliability of doors and locks, lighting and the safety of residents.

Each system has its own obligations. Each must fulfil them properly. And each must have mechanisms for oversight and accountability.

**We investigate systemic failures not to find excuses for those responsible. We investigate them to understand what needs to change so that similar events do not happen again.**

## P.S.

Individual responsibility does not dissolve into systemic responsibility.  
Systemic responsibility does not disappear because of individual guilt.  
These are two separate obligations that must be fulfilled simultaneously.

# The Pattern We Create Together | Living Topography

![_Living Topography. The Pattern We Create Together. AP | Pivtorak.Studio. 17.11.2026_ © Anna Pivtorak (Kostyuk)](/images/living-topography-008-the-pattern-we-create-together.webp)

**17 November 2026 | Meeting № 8 | agirE CLDS 5G, Esposende**

What stayed with me most from this meeting was the way individual movements began to fall into step with one another. At first, it looked like an unfamiliar mechanism at work: one movement followed another, the alternating actions created a rhythm, and each participant had to find their place in the sequence. I instinctively thought of a sewing machine — or, more precisely, an embroidery machine. The association stayed with me as an image of a meeting in which we had begun to create something together.

## When Individual Movements Become One Mechanism

We began the meeting in a circle. Among other things, we became acquainted with the Portuguese words _renda_, _croché_ and _bordado_ — lace, crochet and embroidery.

And then we found ourselves inside a kind of mechanism.

For one of the exercises, we were divided into two groups, A and B, with participants taking turns around the circle. We had to coordinate our hand movements, alternating them so that they interwove. Experiencing the process from within, I suddenly saw something resembling the operation of a sewing machine or an embroidery machine.

I liked this association. Not because I know what the exercise was intended to represent — I don't. It was simply the impression the movements made on me: separate actions repeated in a particular sequence, each depending on the others. If someone dropped out of the rhythm or began acting independently, the overall movement would no longer be the same.

It was not simply a group of people moving their hands at the same time. We had to adjust to one another, sense the sequence and maintain a shared rhythm.

## When Each Person Becomes Someone Else's Support

Another exercise required not so much coordination of our hands as coordination of our whole bodies.

We stood close together in a circle and had to squat down simultaneously, so that each person became a kind of chair for the person in front of them.

At first glance, the task seemed simple. But it was impossible to rely solely on your own sense of balance. We had to take account of other people's positions, the distance between us, the timing of the movement and the way the whole structure changed as everyone began to play their part.

I like the fact that this exercise did not need any verbal explanation of interdependence. It became something we could physically feel.

You do not simply lean on someone else. At the same time, you become part of the support for another person. Whether the whole group can hold that shared position depends not on the strongest participant, but on everyone's ability to coordinate.

## Someone Else's Experience in a Shared Creative Space

Next, participants showed objects connected with handcrafts: napkins, scarves, lace, and toys made by crocheting or knitting. I looked at these things and thought about how many different ways there are to create something by hand. Every craft has its own technique, rhythm and logic for shaping a form.

Later, the group split into two parts. Some participants worked on a theatrical interpretation of Marta's story, while others discussed a possible title for the future performance.

Marta shared her own journey towards independence: how she left her family home to pursue her studies and build a life of her own. It is a story of moving from the familiar surroundings of family life into a new stage, where one gradually has to find one's own path and make independent decisions.

I was interested in the principle behind this work.

One person's personal experience became material for collective creativity. Marta remained the author of her own story, but its stage interpretation was created by the other participants. They listened, tried to understand what they had heard, and sought to express it through theatrical action.

At the end, we watched the other participants perform their interpretation of the story.

There is, perhaps, a particular challenge in this kind of process. What we hear cannot simply be reproduced mechanically. Each person receives it through their own experience, imagination and way of seeing the world. A stage interpretation therefore inevitably becomes an interpretation in another sense, too.

At the same time, it offers an opportunity to approach someone else's experience not merely as a listener, but through one's own creative action.

I think it is important that such work does not require us to become like the person whose story we are interpreting. It requires us to treat what they have shared with care and to find a way of conveying what we have heard without claiming it as our own.

## Togetherness Is Not Created Only According to a Plan

![_Living Topography. The Pattern We Create Together. AP | Pivtorak.Studio. 17.11.2026_ © Anna Pivtorak (Kostyuk)](/images/living-topography-008-the-pattern-we-create-together-01.webp)

The meeting also included quite different moments — things that do not necessarily have a place in the programme, but naturally become part of the time people spend together.

There was pie, coffee and juice — it was simply an ordinary part of human life that people brought into the space where they met.

A shared space gradually comes to hold more than exercises, words and creative tasks. There is room for initiative, everyday gestures, informal conversation and consideration for one another.

## A Pattern That Cannot Be Created Alone

There is no single loop in a piece of lace that could form the entire pattern on its own. Each element has its place, and the overall shape emerges through their connections. Change the way they are interwoven, and the result changes too.

Perhaps that is what I saw in our exercises. First, movements that had to be coordinated. Then, a shared position that could only be maintained together. Finally, creative work in which everyone could contribute, without necessarily doing the same thing as everyone else.

We were not literally making lace. But across these different activities, I saw something akin to its underlying logic: individual elements, the connections between them, and a form that gradually emerges through collective effort.

And perhaps the most interesting thing is that no one can determine in advance exactly what this pattern will look like. It depends on everyone, yet it belongs to no one person alone.

## P.S.

We create a pattern together — movement by movement, gesture by gesture, interaction by interaction.
